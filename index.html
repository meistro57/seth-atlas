The idea

What if reality isn't quite what we think it is?

In the books of Jane Roberts, a personality called Seth describes a remarkable possibility: our beliefs don't merely shape how we experience reality — they may help determine which possibilities a person admits into physical experience. What our investigation found is narrower — and stranger — than that sentence suggests.

IF WE EACH CREATE OUR OWN REALITY
HOW DO WE SHARE THE SAME ONE?

What happens when our beliefs conflict? Who determines what happens — and how is a possibility supposed to become a physical event?

The investigation

A team of AI researchers examined the original texts, reconstructed the proposed mechanisms, and challenged their own interpretations. What emerged wasn't a tidy answer — it was connections, contradictions, competing models, and questions that stayed open. So we built this atlas to explore them.

The invitation

Everything below is connected to the evidence behind it. We're not claiming Seth's ideas are scientifically established. We're asking what the proposed architecture actually says — and where it leaves us wondering.

Beyond Seth: the Missing Bridge question

This atlas began with one question about Seth's account of how experience becomes physical. That question turned out to be unanswerable from the material we had, and the attempt to answer it produced a different investigation — one about why the fields that study unusual experience never compare notes.

The relationship between these two bodies of work runs one way. Seth's mechanisms are what we set out to test. The Missing Bridge work is what happened when we tried: the constructs turned out to be unmeasurable in the available material, the claims we built on them were withdrawn one at a time, and the scope expanded because of that failure rather than in spite of it.

Nothing that follows corroborates any Seth mechanism. No experiment reported here supports Seth's account of reality, and the absence of a refutation is not support. Seth is the premise, not the conclusion.

Three things that get called the same thing

Almost every claim that disparate phenomena share a cause collapses three separate questions into one. They are kept apart throughout this site.

Shared mechanism

One causal process produces several classes of experience. This is the strong claim, and it is the one that requires a generator to be identified, measured, and shown to produce both classes.

Shared trigger

One variable raises the rate of experiences across classes without causing any of them — bad sleep, stress, geomagnetic conditions. A shared trigger is not evidence of a shared phenomenon; it is evidence of a shared observation apparatus, and it points away from unification.

Shared interpretation

One narrative template explains varied experiences after the fact. The experience may stay constant while the label drifts — demon, fairy, alien. This is the weakest claim about reality and the easiest to demonstrate, because it only requires reports to be compared.

Most of what reads as evidence for unification is the third level presented as the first. That distinction is the single most useful thing this investigation produced.

A separate preliminary investigation — Hope's C3 pilot

Conducted independently by Hope, in her own environment, and reported here without integration into this site's causal model.

What was tested: 14 Nostradamus quatrains, assessed against historical events under provisional scoring criteria developed for the exercise.

The strength of this result: 14 claims, self-developed provisional scoring, a methodology that remains under review, and two dated outcomes still awaiting independent historical confirmation. The analysis also identified multiple publication layers, later historical reinterpretations, and a known source of prior translations — all of which complicate any claim that a text predicted an event.

Under those provisional criteria, no novel-information hit was independently verified. That is a property of 14 claims under preliminary scoring — not a verdict on channeling, and not evidence for or against any Seth mechanism. The two investigations ask different questions by different methods, and a null here leaves Seth's account exactly where it was: untested.
